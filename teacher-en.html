How to Become a Teacher: A Guide for Students and Career Changers

Teachers are needed everywhere — in big cities and small towns, in schools, colleges and online. Here's what teachers really do, how to qualify in the US and UK, whether you can switch into teaching from another career, and how to get started right now.

In short: to teach in a public school you usually need a bachelor's degree plus a teacher preparation program and a state license (US), or a degree plus Qualified Teacher Status (UK). Career changers can use alternative routes. The job is about more than knowing your subject — you need to explain clearly, manage a class and connect with very different students. You can test it now by tutoring or running a club.

A great teacher is remembered for life: they can spark a love of a subject — or put students off it for years. That's why teaching takes not only knowledge but patience, empathy and people skills. In return, few jobs offer such a strong sense of purpose: you see your students grow.

What a teacher does

Lessons are only the visible part of the job. A typical teacher:

  • Plans lessons — works out how to explain a topic and chooses tasks and examples.
  • Teaches — introduces new material, answers questions, keeps the class engaged.
  • Grades and gives feedback — on homework, tests and projects.
  • Works with parents — shares progress and problems and finds solutions together.
  • Handles admin — records, plans, reports.
  • Supports students beyond lessons — as a form tutor or homeroom teacher, running activities and helping with non-academic issues.

Teaching is a popular second career. Professionals with real-world experience — engineers, programmers, scientists, translators — often make strong teachers because they know how the subject is used in life. Read more in how to change careers at 30+.

What you need to be good at

1. Knowing your subject

You can only explain what you truly understand. A teacher needs to know the subject beyond the curriculum — to answer unexpected questions and show why it matters.

2. Explaining clearly

Breaking complex ideas into steps, finding real-life examples and checking that students actually understood. This skill grows with practice.

3. Understanding young people

Developmental psychology helps you see why an 11-year-old and a 16-year-old behave differently and how to reach each of them. Read more in how to become a psychologist.

4. Managing a classroom

Keeping the attention of 25–30 students, handling noise and conflict, and creating an atmosphere where people want to learn.

5. Using digital tools

Learning platforms, presentations, online gradebooks and now AI tools for preparing materials are part of everyday teaching.

How to start right now

Tutor someone younger. Explain a tricky topic to a younger sibling or classmate. It's the best way to find out whether you enjoy teaching.
Learn how people learn. Coursera: Learning How to Learn is one of the most popular online courses about how memory and attention work. Free to audit.
Watch how great explainers do it. Khan Academy has free lessons in math and science. Pay attention not just to the topic, but to how it's presented.
Run a session yourself. A club, a workshop for younger students, camp counseling, volunteering at a youth center — any chance to stand on the other side of the desk.

Free resources

  • Coursera: Learning How to Learn — how learning works, free to audit
  • Khan Academy — free video lessons and a model of clear explanation
  • Edutopia — free articles and videos for teachers on teaching strategies

How much teachers earn

Teacher pay depends heavily on country, state or region, experience and type of school:

  • Public schools usually pay on a salary scale that rises with experience and qualifications. Pay is stable and comes with benefits, though it is rarely the highest among graduate jobs.
  • Private and online schools vary widely — some pay more, some less.
  • Private tutoring is paid by the hour; experienced tutors in high-demand subjects such as math, languages and test prep can earn well.

For real figures, check official salary scales for your state or country and local job postings.

Teaching may be for you if...

  • You love explaining things and enjoy the moment someone finally "gets it"
  • You have a favorite subject you could talk about for hours
  • You're patient and can calmly repeat the same thing several times
  • You're comfortable speaking in front of a group
  • You want your work to make a difference to others

It's probably not for you if noise and constant interaction drain you quickly. Then consider working with a subject without a classroom — instructional designer, curriculum writer, technical writer — or one-on-one tutoring.

FAQ

Can I become a teacher without a teaching degree?

Often, yes. In the US, many states offer alternative certification for people with a bachelor's degree in another field. In the UK, graduates can gain Qualified Teacher Status through postgraduate training such as a PGCE or school-based routes. Tutoring and many online teaching jobs don't require a teaching qualification at all, though training in teaching methods helps a lot.

How long does it take to become a teacher?

Usually about 4–5 years from the start of university, including a degree and teacher training. If you already have a degree, postgraduate or alternative routes typically take 1–2 years.

Is teaching a good career for introverts?

It can be, but a lesson means constant interaction with a group. Many introverts are more comfortable with one-on-one tutoring or online teaching. See more options in best jobs for introverts.

Will AI replace teachers?

AI already helps prepare materials and grade work, but replacing teachers is much harder: students need a person who motivates them, supports them and understands why they're struggling. Teaching is one of the fields where job growth is expected in the coming years. Read more in jobs of the future and AI.
