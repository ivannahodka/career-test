How to Become a Business Analyst: A Guide for Teens

A business analyst is the translator between a business and the team that does the work: figuring out what problem the company actually has, and turning it into something the rest of the team can understand and act on. If you like figuring out "how things really work" and bringing order to chaos, keep reading.

TL;DR: Business analysts gather requirements from stakeholders, break down business processes, and turn vague requests into clear tasks for a team. You start with confident Excel skills and the ability to ask the right questions — coding isn't required.

A business analyst is a specialist who works at the intersection of business and delivery: talking to stakeholders, managers, and clients, figuring out what they actually need, and translating that into concrete requirements — often for developers, data analysts, or other teams to build. This isn't the same as a Data Analyst: that role digs deep into the data itself using SQL and Python, while a business analyst works a level above that — with processes, requirements, and people.

What a business analyst actually does

A business analyst's work is built around constant dialogue between different sides and turning that dialogue into a clear plan:

  • Gathers requirements — talks to stakeholders and employees to understand exactly what problem needs solving.
  • Analyzes business processes — maps how work happens today and finds what can be improved or sped up.
  • Writes requirements documentation — translates vague requests into clear, testable tasks for the team.
  • Connects the different sides — explains to the delivery team what the business wants, and to leadership what's actually feasible and how long it will take.

A good business analyst listens more than they talk — often the most valuable part of the job happens before a single line of a technical spec is written.

What to learn

1. Requirements gathering and stakeholder work

How to ask questions that surface the real problem, not just what a stakeholder happened to say the first time — the actual need is often different from how it was first described.

2. Business process analysis and mapping

The ability to sketch how a process works today (like placing an order or handling a support request), spot the bottlenecks, and propose how to make it faster or simpler.

3. Writing documentation

Requirements need to be written so that both the stakeholder and the person building the solution understand them the same way — an ambiguous requirement at this stage almost always leads to rework later.

How much business analysts earn

A business analyst's salary grows with experience and the scale of the projects they work on:

  • Junior business analyst — $800 to $1,500/month: gathering simple requirements and helping with documentation under a mentor's guidance.
  • Mid-level business analyst — $1,500 to $2,500/month: independently managing project requirements and stakeholder communication.
  • Senior / Lead Business Analyst — $2,500 to $3,500/month and up: working on large projects and mentoring junior analysts.

Business analysts are needed not just at tech companies, but in banking, consulting, and retail — anywhere there are complex processes that need to be simplified and explained.

Business analysis might be for you if...

  • You enjoy figuring out "how things actually work" rather than just following ready-made instructions
  • You listen carefully and notice when someone says one thing but means another
  • You're drawn to bringing order to messy processes and finding their weak points
  • You're equally comfortable talking to leadership and to the people doing the work

It's probably not for you if you'd rather dig deep into numbers and code yourself than talk to people — in that case, look at Data Analyst or software development instead.

Frequently asked questions

How is a business analyst different from a Data Analyst?

A Data Analyst digs deep into the data itself — writing SQL queries, processing tables in Python, finding statistical patterns. A business analyst works a level above that: talking to stakeholders and the team, figuring out what business problem is really behind a task, and translating it into clear requirements — often for a data analyst or developers to act on.

Do you need to know how to code?

No, coding isn't a required skill for a business analyst. Confident Excel use, the ability to ask structured questions, and writing clear documentation matter far more than writing code.

What's the best way to start at 14–16?

Try breaking down any process around you — like how sign-ups for a school club work, or the line at a cafeteria — and sketch out where it's slow or confusing. That's the exact same skill a business analyst uses at a company, just on a smaller scale.

Can you start without a relevant degree?

Yes, many business analysts move into the role from adjacent jobs — support, sales, project coordination — bringing an understanding of how the business actually works. A formal degree matters less than the ability to listen to people and frame problems clearly.
