How to Become a System Administrator: A Guide for Teens

A system administrator is the person who fixes the office internet when nobody's looking, and makes sure servers don't crash in the middle of the night. If you're curious about how all of it works under the hood, keep reading.

A System Administrator is a specialist who sets up, maintains, and secures computer infrastructure: servers, networks, employee workstations, and data backups. If a company's website runs without a hitch and employees can print documents and check email without worry, that's a sysadmin's work — even though it usually goes unnoticed.

What a System Administrator does

If backend and frontend are the kitchen and dining room of a restaurant, a system administrator is the person who keeps the electricity, plumbing, and locks working throughout the whole building so the restaurant can even open:

  • Sets up and maintains servers — installs operating systems and updates, keeps everything running stably.
  • Manages the network — internet access, the office's local network, permissions, VPNs.
  • Makes backups — so data isn't lost for good after a failure or an attack.
  • Responds to incidents — a server goes down, someone's email stops working, a disk fills up — the sysadmin investigates and fixes it.

In small companies, one sysadmin is responsible for literally everything tech-related. In larger ones, they specialize in one area: servers, networking, or security.

What you need to learn

1. Linux and command-line basics

The vast majority of servers in the world run Linux. Being comfortable in the terminal — reading logs, managing processes, setting permissions — is something you simply can't skip on the way to becoming a sysadmin.

2. Networking basics

How the internet actually works: IP addresses, DNS, protocols, ports, firewalls. Without understanding networking, you can't diagnose why a service is unreachable or why two computers can't "see" each other.

3. Automation and scripting

A good sysadmin doesn't repeat the same manual task every day — they write a small Bash or Python script that does it automatically instead.

How much they earn

System administrators are needed by almost any company with its own infrastructure — from a small office to a full data center:

  • Junior — entry level, usually solid Linux and networking basics are enough.
  • Middle — comfortable pay once you have experience maintaining real infrastructure and resolving incidents quickly.
  • Senior / Infrastructure Lead — one of the consistently well-paid tracks, especially with experience running large server infrastructures.

Sysadmins who also pick up automation and cloud platforms naturally move into DevOps roles, where pay is typically even higher.

System administration is for you if...

  • You enjoy figuring out why something isn't working and finding the fix yourself
  • You're not intimidated by working in the command line instead of familiar windows and buttons
  • You're okay with being on call when something breaks urgently
  • You're more interested in the infrastructure "under the hood" than in what a regular user sees

Probably not for you if seeing visual, immediate results matters most to you — in that case, look into frontend development or design instead.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between a system administrator and a DevOps engineer?

A sysadmin usually manages a company's infrastructure as a whole — servers, networks, employee workstations, backups. A DevOps engineer focuses on the infrastructure of one specific software product — automating code deployment, CI/CD, cloud servers. In practice, a sysadmin often grows into a DevOps engineer once they start focusing on one specific product instead of an entire office's infrastructure.

Do I need to know programming?

Full programming — no. But without basic scripting skills (Bash, later Python), a sysadmin's job gets much harder: routine tasks that repeat every day are usually automated with small scripts.

Which operating system should I learn first?

Linux. The vast majority of servers on the internet run Linux, and almost all sysadmin work on the web and in the cloud revolves around the Linux command line. Windows Server is studied separately if the job involves Windows-based corporate infrastructure.

Can I start at 13-14 years old?

Yes. Installing Linux on an old computer or in a virtual machine and starting to learn the command line is free and requires no special equipment — for example, through free hands-on trainers like OverTheWire.
