Jobs of the Future: What Will Be in Demand by 2030 and How AI Is Changing Careers

Artificial intelligence is reshaping the job market faster than ever: some tasks are being automated while other careers are growing. Here's which jobs will be needed in the coming years, which are shrinking, which skills matter most — and how to choose a direction without chasing hype.

Updated: October 2026

In short: demand is growing fastest for specialists in data, AI, cybersecurity and software, while the largest numbers of new jobs are in care, education, construction and logistics. Routine office roles are declining. AI more often changes the tasks within a job than replaces it entirely, so analytical thinking, adaptability and a habit of lifelong learning matter most.

What's happening to the job market

Every few years the World Economic Forum publishes the Future of Jobs report, based on a survey of more than a thousand large employers worldwide. According to the 2025 edition, by 2030:

  • about 170 million new jobs will be created;
  • about 92 million will be displaced;
  • a net gain of roughly 78 million jobs;
  • around 39% of workers' core skills are expected to change.

The main drivers are technology (especially AI and data processing), the green transition, ageing populations in some countries and growing young populations in others.

Jobs with the most new positions

Growth rate isn't the same as the number of openings. In absolute numbers, the most new jobs will appear in roles that are hard to automate:

  • Care and health — nurses, social workers, personal care aides. Many countries' populations are ageing.
  • Education — teachers, especially at secondary level, and tutors.
  • Construction and manufacturing — builders, technicians, engineers.
  • Logistics and retail — delivery drivers, shop salespeople, farmworkers.

Jobs that are declining

Demand is falling fastest for routine tasks that are easy to automate:

  • cashiers and bank tellers;
  • data entry clerks;
  • administrative assistants and executive secretaries;
  • accounting, bookkeeping and payroll clerks;
  • postal service clerks and printing workers.
"Declining" doesn't mean "gone tomorrow". The report also lists graphic designers among declining roles — generative AI now handles simple layouts and banners. But demand for strong designers who understand business goals, build brand identities and work well with AI tools hasn't disappeared. What changes is what the job expects of you. How AI is changing careers

AI rarely replaces a whole job — more often it takes over individual tasks. A programmer writes code faster with an AI assistant but still decides what to build and why. A marketer generates copy variations but sets the strategy. A doctor gets help reading scans but makes the diagnosis and talks to the patient.

Hence a simple rule: the more routine, template-based work a job involves, the more it will change. The more it involves human interaction, responsibility for decisions, creativity and hands-on work in unpredictable settings, the more resilient it is.

Skills of the future

Employers in the report name analytical thinking as the most important skill. Next come:

  • Resilience, flexibility and agility — staying calm through change and adapting quickly.
  • Leadership and social influence — negotiating, leading a team, persuading.
  • Creative thinking — coming up with things that don't exist yet.
  • Curiosity and lifelong learning — careers will keep changing throughout your working life.

The fastest-growing skills are AI and big data, networks and cybersecurity, and general technological literacy. This isn't just for tech people: knowing how to use AI tools will help teachers, lawyers and designers alike.

How to choose a future-proof career

Start with yourself, not a ranking. The most in-demand job won't bring success if it doesn't interest you. Take a career test to see which fields suit you.
Check how AI is changing your chosen field. Which tasks are already being automated, and which stay with people? Aim for the part of the work where humans are needed.
Learn the AI tools in your field. A designer with AI works faster than one without it. The same goes for almost any career.
Build "human" skills. Communication, argument, teamwork, ownership of decisions — the things AI does worst.

FAQ

Which jobs will AI replace?

Mainly those with many repetitive, template-based tasks: data entry, cashier work, routine bookkeeping and secretarial work, standard copy and layouts. But more often AI doesn't replace a job entirely — it changes it: some tasks are automated and people move to more complex work.

Which jobs won't AI replace?

The most resilient are jobs built on human interaction, care, responsibility for decisions and hands-on work in unpredictable settings: doctors and nurses, psychologists, teachers, social workers, electricians and builders, managers. AI can help them but not replace them.

Is it still worth going into tech?

Technology roles are still among the fastest-growing, especially data, AI and cybersecurity. But it's harder for beginners: simple tasks are being automated and employers expect more skills from day one. Choose tech if you're genuinely interested in how technology works, not just for the salaries.

What should students learn to stay employable?

Math and logic for analytical thinking. English, since most knowledge and tools are in it. The basics of working with AI and data. And "human" skills: speaking, writing, teamwork. Above all, choose a direction that interests you, because you'll be learning all the time.
