How to Become a Surgeon: Training, Specialties and Skills

A surgeon is a doctor who treats disease and injury through operations. It's one of the most demanding and responsible careers in medicine: long training, heavy workloads and decisions that directly affect people's lives. Here's how to become a surgeon in the US, UK and India, how long it takes, which surgical specialties exist and how to tell in high school whether it's right for you.

In short: becoming a surgeon means first becoming a doctor and then completing several years of surgical training — in total usually 12–15 years or more after high school. Chemistry and biology are the key subjects. The most important qualities are steady hands, stamina, composure under stress and quick decision-making.

Surgeons don't only operate. They examine patients, make diagnoses, decide whether surgery is needed, prepare patients for it and then follow their recovery. Many operations are planned, but some are emergencies at night or on weekends. That's why surgery takes not only knowledge but a particular temperament.

What a surgeon does

  • Examines and diagnoses — using tests and scans to decide whether surgery is needed.
  • Plans operations — chooses the approach and discusses risks with the patient and team.
  • Operates — from minor procedures to operations lasting many hours.
  • Cares for patients after surgery — ward rounds, wound care, watching for complications.
  • Takes on-call shifts — handling emergencies such as trauma, bleeding or appendicitis.
  • Works in a team — with anesthesiologists, theatre nurses and other doctors.

Today many operations are done through small incisions with a camera (laparoscopy) or with robotic systems, so modern surgeons also need to be comfortable with technology.

Surgery is a marathon. From the first day of medical school to confident independent practice usually takes well over a decade. That's normal: surgical skill grows with every operation, and experienced surgeons keep learning throughout their careers.

A day in the life of a surgeon

  • Morning — team handover, ward rounds, checking wounds.
  • Day — planned operations, each lasting from one to several hours.
  • After surgery — notes, talking with patients and families, consulting on other wards.
  • On call — several times a month, staying overnight to handle emergencies.

What you need to be good at

  • Steady hands. Fine motor skills and coordination are the foundation of the job.
  • Stamina. Operations last for hours and you work standing up.
  • Composure. Unexpected things happen during surgery, and you need to decide quickly and calmly.
  • Spatial thinking. You need to picture anatomy in 3D.
  • Teamwork. An operation is done by a whole team, not one person.
  • Responsibility. The surgeon is accountable for the outcome and the patient's recovery.

How to start right now

Strengthen chemistry and biology. They're the core of medical school admissions.
Train your hands. Model building, crafts, sewing, playing a musical instrument — anything that builds precision and coordination.
Learn anatomy. OpenStax: Anatomy and Physiology 2e is a free university-level textbook.
Take a first aid course. You'll see how you react to blood and emergencies — important for a future surgeon.
Stay active. Stamina matters: long operations require good physical fitness.

How much surgeons earn

Surgeons' pay depends on country, specialty, experience and employer:

  • During residency or junior training, pay is modest compared with later.
  • Fully trained surgeons are among the highest earners in medicine in most countries.
  • Some specialties, such as neurosurgery, orthopedics and cardiothoracic surgery, tend to pay more.
  • Private practice often pays more than public hospitals.

For real figures, check official sources such as the US Bureau of Labor Statistics or NHS pay scales in the UK.

Surgery may be for you if...

  • You enjoy working with your hands and seeing quick results
  • You stay calm under pressure and the sight of blood doesn't bother you
  • You have good stamina and can handle irregular hours
  • Chemistry and biology go reasonably well for you
  • You're ready to train for more than a decade

It's probably not for you if you want a calm schedule without night shifts. Consider other medical specialties or dentistry, which is also very hands-on but has few night shifts.

FAQ

How many years does it take to become a surgeon?

Usually 12–15 years or more after high school. In the US that's 4 years of college, 4 years of medical school and at least 5 years of residency. In the UK it's a 5–6-year medical degree, 2 foundation years and around 8 years of surgical training. Some specialties take longer.

What subjects do I need to become a surgeon?

Chemistry and biology are essential for medical school, and math and physics help. You don't choose surgery at the admissions stage — you decide on a surgical specialty during or after medical school.

Can women become surgeons?

Absolutely. The number of women in surgery keeps growing across every specialty, from general surgery to neurosurgery. Skills, knowledge and stamina matter — not gender.

What if I'm afraid of blood?

Many medical students feel uncomfortable at first, and it usually fades with exposure. You can test yourself through a first aid course or hospital volunteering. If the fear is strong and doesn't go away, consider medical specialties that don't involve surgery.
