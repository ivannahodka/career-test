What Career to Choose After School: A Guide for Ages 16 and 18

Deciding what to do after school can feel like a choice for life — and that makes it scary. In reality it's a first step you can adjust later. Here's what your options are at 16 and at 18, how they work in the US, the UK and India, how to figure out what suits you, and the mistakes students make most often.

In short: most school systems have two big decision points — around 16, when you choose subjects, a stream or a vocational route, and around 18, when you choose between university, college, an apprenticeship or work. Don't pick the "most prestigious" career — pick one where your interests, strengths and the real day-to-day work overlap. Start with a test, then check your choice in practice.

Many students put this off until application deadlines, then choose a course by entry requirements or follow their friends. It's better to spend a few weeks on it now: understand yourself, look at careers from the inside, and only then decide which subjects to take and where to apply.

Two decision points: at 16 and at 18

School systems have different names for the stages, but the logic is similar almost everywhere:

CountryAround age 16Around age 18
🇬🇧 EnglandAfter GCSEs (end of Year 11): A-levels at sixth form or college, T-levels, BTECs or an apprenticeshipAfter Year 13: university, a degree apprenticeship, higher technical qualifications or work
🇺🇸 USAIn high school (grades 9–12): choosing electives, AP or honors classes, career and technical education (CTE) programsAfter 12th grade: a 4-year college, community college, trade school, an apprenticeship or work
🇮🇳 IndiaAfter Class 10: choosing a stream — Science, Commerce or Arts/Humanities — or a polytechnic diplomaAfter Class 12: entrance exams and a degree, or a diploma and work

In Russia and other CIS countries the same choices happen after 9th and 11th grade — see the Russian version of this guide.

Your options at 16

  • Academic route — A-levels in the UK, AP/honors classes in the US, the Science or Commerce stream in India. It keeps the most doors open and prepares you for university.
  • Technical or vocational route — T-levels and BTECs in the UK, CTE programs in US high schools, polytechnic diplomas in India. You study a specific field — IT, engineering, healthcare, design, business — with a lot of practice.
  • Apprenticeship (mainly UK and Europe) — you work, earn a wage and study at the same time. Good if you learn best by doing.

At 16 you rarely need to pick an exact job. What matters is choosing subjects that keep the right doors open: for example, medicine usually requires chemistry and biology, and engineering requires math and physics.

The vocational route doesn't close the door to university. In the UK, T-levels and BTECs carry UCAS points; in the US, many students start at a community college and transfer to a 4-year university. You can start practical and go academic later.

Your options at 18

  • University — required for doctors, lawyers, scientists, most engineers and teachers.
  • Community college or college — shorter, cheaper programs that lead to a job or a transfer to university.
  • Degree apprenticeship or trade school — a qualification plus paid work experience, often with little or no student debt.
  • Courses and self-study — in IT, design, marketing and copywriting, employers often care about your portfolio and skills more than your diploma.
  • Gap year or work — a year of work, volunteering or travel can help you decide, as long as you use it to try things, not just to wait.

How to choose a career: 5 steps

Understand your interests. Write down what you like doing when nobody makes you: figuring out gadgets, drawing, helping people, debating, working with numbers, building things. Answer honestly: "What could I talk about for hours?"
Find your strengths. Which subjects come easier than others? What do friends and teachers praise you for? Interest plus ability is the most reliable basis for a choice.
Take a career test. It narrows the field: instead of hundreds of careers you get a few directions worth exploring. Our test is free and needs no sign-up.
Look at the job from the inside. Read about a real working day, watch videos by people in the field, ask to shadow someone for a day. A job often looks very different from how it's shown in movies.
Try it in practice. A free online course, a club, a competition, volunteering, work experience, a small project of your own. A month of practice tells you more than a year of thinking.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing to stay with friends. Your friend has different interests and abilities. What suits them won't necessarily suit you.
  • Choosing only for prestige or money. A high salary won't save you from boredom if the work isn't interesting. And in any field, good specialists earn more than average ones.
  • Letting parents choose for you. Their advice is valuable — they know you and have life experience. But you're the one who will do the work. Discuss your reasons with them instead of just agreeing.
  • Confusing a subject with a job. Loving biology doesn't mean you'll enjoy being a doctor: that also means long shifts, responsibility and talking to patients. Find out what the work really looks like.
  • Dropping subjects too early. Many courses require specific subjects at 16–18. If you're unsure, keep math and at least one science — they open the most doors.
Don't be afraid to get it wrong. People change careers at 25 and at 40. The skills you gain are almost never wasted: analytical thinking, knowing how to learn and how to work in a team are useful everywhere.

FAQ

Should I go to university or do an apprenticeship?

It depends on the career and how you learn best. Some careers — medicine, law, research — require a degree. In many others, such as IT, engineering, business and skilled trades, apprenticeships and vocational programs are a strong alternative: you earn while you learn and gain real experience early. You can also start with an apprenticeship and study for a degree later.

Do I need a degree to get a good job?

Not always. In IT, design, marketing and many trades, employers increasingly look at skills, portfolios and experience. But for regulated professions like doctor, lawyer, teacher or engineer, a degree is usually required. Check the entry requirements of the specific career you're considering.

What if nothing interests me?

That's normal for many teenagers — you simply haven't tried much yet. Start with a career test to see directions you hadn't considered, then try two or three of them in practice: a free course, a club, work experience. Interest often appears along the way, not before.

Can I change careers if I choose wrong?

Yes. You can switch courses, retrain, take a conversion course or learn new skills online. Many successful people didn't find their field on the first try — and experience from a previous career often becomes their advantage.
