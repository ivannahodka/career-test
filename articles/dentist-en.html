How to Become a Dentist: Education, Specialties and First Steps

A dentist is a doctor who treats teeth, gums and the whole mouth. It's one of the most in-demand healthcare careers — everyone needs a dentist, from young children to older adults. Here's how dentistry differs from other medical careers, how long training takes in the US, UK and India, which specialties exist and how to tell in high school whether it suits you.

In short: to become a dentist you need a dental degree — a DDS or DMD in the US after college, or a BDS in the UK and India. Training usually takes about 5–8 years in total, plus more for specialties such as orthodontics or oral surgery. Chemistry and biology matter most for admission. Beyond knowledge, you need steady hands and the ability to put anxious patients at ease.

Dentistry is a special part of medicine. Most of the work is done with your hands, using precise instruments and equipment, and you see the result immediately: a patient leaves pain-free and with a better smile. At the same time, a dentist is a fully trained clinician who understands anatomy, pharmacology and how oral health connects to the health of the whole body.

What a dentist does

  • Examines and diagnoses — finds cavities, gum disease and bite problems, reads X-rays.
  • Treats teeth — places fillings, performs root canals, removes tartar.
  • Extracts teeth and performs surgery — for example removing wisdom teeth or placing implants.
  • Restores teeth — fits crowns, bridges and dentures.
  • Corrects bites — plans braces or clear aligners.
  • Prevents problems — teaches patients how to care for their teeth and does professional cleanings.

There are shorter routes into dentistry. Dental hygienists, dental assistants and dental technicians (who make crowns and dentures in a lab) train for much less time. These roles let you work in dental care sooner — and test whether you'd enjoy becoming a dentist later.

How dentistry differs from other medical careers

  • Training is often a little shorter than for many medical specialties.
  • The work is mostly hands-on. Many describe dentistry as precision craftsmanship.
  • Few night shifts — most dentists work regular clinic hours.
  • Lots of private practice. Many dentists work in private clinics or run their own, so business skills are useful too.
  • A specific physical load — long hours in one position strain the back, neck and eyes.

What you need to be good at

  • Steady hands and fine motor skills. You work on a millimeter scale.
  • Spatial thinking. You need to picture teeth and jaws in 3D, often while working through a mirror.
  • Patience and precision. Some procedures take hours.
  • Putting patients at ease. Many people are afraid of the dentist, and how you communicate decides whether they come back.
  • An eye for aesthetics. Especially important in cosmetic and restorative dentistry.

How to start right now

Strengthen chemistry and biology. Dental school places are highly competitive.
Train your hands. Sculpting, model building, woodworking, drawing — anything that builds precision and fine motor skills.
Read about anatomy. OpenStax: Anatomy and Physiology 2e is a free university-level textbook.
See the job from the inside. Ask your own dentist a few questions about the profession, shadow at a dental clinic if possible, or attend a dental school open day.

How much dentists earn

Dentists' pay depends on country, specialty, experience and setting:

  • Employed dentists in public services earn a stable salary.
  • In private practice, pay is often a share of treatment fees, so income depends on patient numbers and the complexity of work.
  • Specialists such as orthodontists and oral surgeons generally earn more.
  • Practice owners can earn well, but need investment and management skills.

For real figures, check official sources such as the US Bureau of Labor Statistics or NHS pay information in the UK.

Dentistry may be for you if...

  • You enjoy working with your hands and doing things very precisely
  • Chemistry and biology go reasonably well for you
  • You're careful, patient and notice small details
  • You like seeing the result of your work right away
  • You can stay calm and kind with people who are nervous or scared

It's probably not for you if sitting in one position for a long time and doing meticulous work wears you out. If you want a career in medicine with more varied tasks, read the general guide how to become a doctor.

FAQ

How many years does it take to become a dentist?

In the US, usually 8 years: a 4-year bachelor's degree and 4 years of dental school. In the UK and India, a BDS takes about 5 years. Specialties such as orthodontics or oral surgery add several more years of training.

What subjects do I need to become a dentist?

Chemistry and biology are almost always required, and physics and math are often recommended. Competition for dental school is high, so focus on these subjects early and check each university's exact requirements.

What's the difference between a dentist and a dental hygienist?

A dentist is a doctor with a dental degree who diagnoses problems and performs treatments such as fillings, extractions and root canals. A dental hygienist has shorter training and focuses on cleaning teeth, prevention and patient education, usually working alongside a dentist.

Do I need to be good at art to become a dentist?

Not necessarily, but strong spatial thinking and steady hands help a lot. Drawing, sculpting and model building develop exactly these skills, which is why many future dentists enjoy them in school.
