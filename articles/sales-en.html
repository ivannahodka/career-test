How to Become a Sales Manager: A Guide for Teens

A sales manager helps a customer see how a company's product solves their specific problem, and carries that understanding through to an actual deal. It's one of the few careers where results are immediately measurable in money, and you can start practicing right now — no degree or specialized education required.

TL;DR: Sales managers build client relationships and close deals. You start with basic communication and objection-handling skills — CRM systems, pipeline management, and key account work come next.

A sales manager (or account executive) is the face of the company to the client: finding potential buyers, showing them the product, answering questions and doubts, and ultimately carrying the deal through to payment. Unlike a marketer, who works with a large audience at once, a salesperson works one-on-one with a specific person or company.

What a sales manager actually does

Sales work revolves around constant communication with people and the ability to carry a conversation through to a result:

  • Finds clients — sources potential buyers through cold calls, social media, referrals, or inbound leads.
  • Runs presentations and demos — shows the product in a way that helps the client see it as a solution to their specific problem.
  • Handles objections — responds to a client's doubts ("too expensive," "we don't need this") not by pushing, but by understanding the real reason behind them.
  • Closes deals — prepares a commercial proposal and carries the client through to signing a contract or making a payment.

A good salesperson doesn't "push" a sale — they honestly help the client decide whether the product is right for them. In the long run, that approach brings more repeat business and referrals.

What to learn

1. Negotiation and objection-handling basics

How to listen to a client, ask open-ended questions, uncover their real need, and stay calm in the face of rejection — this is the foundation without which no serious deal can be closed.

2. Working with CRM systems

A CRM (Customer Relationship Management) tool — like HubSpot CRM or Pipedrive — is software for tracking clients and deals. Being able to manage a sales pipeline in a CRM helps you avoid losing clients and see exactly where each deal stands.

3. Understanding the product and the client

A salesperson needs to know the company's product better than most other employees — otherwise it's impossible to explain how it helps this specific client, rather than some abstract "average buyer."

Where to start right now

Read a sales classic — Neil Rackham's "SPIN Selling" explains how to ask questions that naturally lead a client toward the decision to buy.
Learn a free CRM — create an account with HubSpot CRM (free tier) and practice managing an imaginary sales pipeline.
Read practical articles — HubSpot Sales Blog — a free blog breaking down real sales and negotiation techniques.
Practice a live call — ask a friend to play a "client with objections" and try to carry the conversation through to a mock "deal," then review what worked.

Start small. Try selling something real — your own services, things you no longer need, tickets to a school event. Even that small amount of experience teaches you to explain value and negotiate — exactly what a salesperson does every day.

How much sales managers earn

Sales salaries are usually a base pay plus a commission on deals, so income depends a lot on results:

  • Junior sales manager — $600 to $1,200/month (base + small commission): first calls and deals under a mentor's guidance.
  • Mid-level sales manager — $1,200 to $2,500/month: independently managing clients with a steady commission on closed deals.
  • Senior / Key Account Manager — $2,500 to $4,000/month and up: working with major clients, where the commission on a single deal can be substantial.

Because of the commission structure, the income ceiling in this career is often higher than in other business roles at the same level — but it's also less stable, since income depends directly on the number of deals closed.

Sales might be for you if...

  • You're comfortable talking to strangers and pitching them something
  • You're not afraid of rejection and can try again after hearing "no"
  • You enjoy persuading and negotiating rather than just following instructions
  • You're motivated by a direct link between your effort and your income

It's probably not for you if rejection and constant people-interaction wear you out emotionally — in that case, look at analytics or finance, where there's more independent work with data.

Frequently asked questions

How is a sales manager different from a marketer?

A marketer draws attention to a product and generates interest among a large audience. A sales manager works with one specific person or company at a time — taking an already interested lead through to a closed deal, answering questions, and handling the close.

Do you need to be a "born salesperson"?

No — it's primarily a set of specific skills, like listening to the client, asking the right questions, and handling objections, rather than an innate talent. It's learned the same way as any other profession, through practice and reviewing your own calls.

What's the best way to start at 14–16?

Try selling something real — your own services (tutoring, helping with something), things you no longer need, or tickets to a school event. Even that small amount of experience teaches you to explain value and negotiate with people.

Can you start without a college degree?

Yes, sales is one of the few careers where a degree barely matters — real results and the ability to close deals count for much more. Many successful sales managers started without a relevant degree, learning entirely on the job.
