How to Become a UX/UI Designer: A Guide for Teens

A UX/UI designer decides why some apps make sense the moment you open them, while others make you want to close your phone in frustration. If you're curious about how usable interfaces get made, keep reading.

A UX/UI Designer is a specialist who plans how an app or website's interface looks and works: screens, buttons, the flow between them, the logic of what a user does next. UX (User Experience) is about usability and clarity; UI (User Interface) is about visual appearance — colors, fonts, icons. Unlike a programmer, a designer doesn't write code — they produce a mockup, a detailed blueprint of the interface that developers then build.

What a UX/UI Designer does

If backend is a restaurant's kitchen and frontend is the dining room where it's all served, a UX/UI designer is the architect who, before the place even opens, figures out how a guest will easily find a table, the menu, and the exit:

  • Studies users — their goals, what frustrates them, where they get lost in an interface.
  • Designs screen logic — which button leads where, what happens after a tap, how to avoid confusing people.
  • Builds mockups in Figma — from simple boxes (wireframes) to a finished visual design with colors and typography.
  • Tests solutions — shows the mockup to real people and watches whether they can complete a task without hints.

A UX/UI designer works closely with developers and a product manager: the designer figures out what should happen, and developers turn it into working code.

What you need to learn

1. The difference between UX and UI

UX is structure and logic: which screens are needed, in what order, what should happen if a user taps the wrong thing. UI is how all of that looks visually. Beginners often mix up these levels and jump straight to coloring buttons without first figuring out why those buttons exist in that order at all.

2. The tool — Figma

Figma is a free interface design editor that runs right in the browser and has become the industry standard. It's used for everything: from quick sketches (wireframes) to a final mockup with ready-made components that get handed off to a developer.

3. Usability principles and the psychology of perception

A good interface mostly relies not on taste, but on consistent patterns in how people process information: why fewer choices aren't always better, why consistency between screens matters, and how a user's memory and attention actually work.

How much they earn

Almost any product with a website or app needs UX/UI designers, and there's a shortage of good ones:

  • Junior — entry level, usually a portfolio of a couple of practice projects in Figma is enough.
  • Middle — comfortable pay once you have experience working on real products with a dev team.
  • Senior / Lead Designer — one of the consistently well-paid tracks, especially with product-company experience.

Designers who can also run user research (UX research) are valued even more highly — there are noticeably fewer of them on the market.

UX/UI design is for you if...

  • You notice when something in an app feels off, and immediately think about how you'd fix it
  • You like putting things in order — sorting, structuring, planning logic
  • You're curious about why people behave the way they do
  • You enjoy both figuring out structure and polishing the final visual result

Probably not for you if you'd rather work with precise logic and numbers than with other people's perception and taste — in that case, look into development or data analytics instead.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between UX and UI?

UX (User Experience) is about how easy and clear a product is to use: screen logic, the user's path, avoiding confusion. UI (User Interface) is about how it looks: colors, fonts, buttons, icons. UX answers "does this work", UI answers "does this look good". A good designer usually understands both, even when specializing in one.

Do I need to be good at drawing?

No. UX/UI design isn't freehand drawing — it's working with ready-made blocks, grids, and typography in tools like Figma. Visual taste develops with practice; it's not a required starting skill.

What tools do I need to get started?

A free Figma account is enough — it's now the industry-standard tool. It runs right in the browser, with no installation or powerful computer required.

Can I start at 13-14 years old?

Yes. Figma is free and browser-based, and the basic principles of design (like the laws of UX) can be learned from free materials with no special preparation.
