How to Become a Doctor: From High School to Your First Job

Being a doctor is one of the most respected — and most demanding — careers: the training is long and the responsibility is huge, but the sense of purpose is hard to match. Here's how medical specialties differ, how long training takes in the US, UK and India, which subjects matter and how to find out in high school whether medicine is right for you.

In short: becoming a doctor takes a medical degree plus several years of supervised specialty training — roughly 10–15 years in total, depending on the country and specialty. Chemistry and biology are the key subjects. If you want to work in healthcare sooner, nursing, paramedic and other allied health careers are shorter routes.

A doctor doesn't just diagnose and treat. They talk with patients and families, make decisions under uncertainty, work in teams with nurses and other specialists, and keep learning throughout their careers because medicine changes every year. That's why knowledge, stamina and people skills matter equally.

Types of doctors

"Doctor" covers dozens of different jobs. A surgeon's day looks very little like a psychiatrist's:

If you'd rather help people through conversation than medication, look at becoming a psychologist — the training and the job are different.

Want to work in healthcare sooner? Nursing, paramedic, physician assistant and other allied health careers take less time to train for. They're also a great way to test yourself in medicine before committing to the long road to becoming a doctor.

Which subjects matter

Almost everywhere, chemistry and biology are essential for medical school, and math and physics help too. Competition for places is high, so start strengthening these subjects early — ideally by age 14–15. Check the exact entry requirements of the universities you're interested in.

What you need besides knowledge

  • Stamina. Long training, night shifts and many hours on your feet.
  • Resilience under stress. Doctors make decisions that affect people's health and lives and face difficult situations.
  • Communication. Explaining a diagnosis in plain language, reassuring patients and breaking bad news are part of the job.
  • Attention to detail. A wrong dose or a missed symptom can be costly.
  • Lifelong learning. Medicine develops very fast.

How to start right now

Strengthen chemistry and biology. They're the basis for both admissions and medical school. Science competitions and advanced classes help too.
Read an anatomy textbook. OpenStax: Anatomy and Physiology 2e is a free university-level textbook.
Watch medical lessons. Khan Academy: Health and Medicine has free videos on how body systems and diseases work.
Take a first aid course. Red Cross organizations and many training centers offer them. You'll see how you react in emergencies — and gain a skill that's useful either way.
See the job from the inside. Volunteer at a hospital or care home, shadow a doctor, or attend an open day at a medical school.

How much doctors earn

Doctors' pay depends heavily on country, specialty, experience and employer:

  • During residency or junior training, pay is usually modest compared with what comes later.
  • Fully qualified doctors are among the higher earners in most countries, especially surgeons and many specialists.
  • Public vs private — private practice often pays more, and many doctors combine both.

For real figures, check official sources such as the US Bureau of Labor Statistics or NHS pay scales in the UK.

Medicine may be for you if...

  • You're curious about how the human body works and why it gets sick
  • Chemistry and biology go reasonably well — or you even enjoy them
  • You're ready for a long education of 10 years or more
  • You stay calm in tough situations and the sight of blood doesn't put you off
  • You want your work to make a real difference to people

It's probably not for you if you want a quick qualification or find night shifts and stress very hard. Then consider related careers: pharmacist, biotechnologist, lab technician, psychologist — or nursing and allied health.

FAQ

How many years does it take to become a doctor?

Usually about 10–15 years. In the US that means 4 years of college, 4 years of medical school and 3–7 years of residency. In the UK it's typically a 5–6-year degree, a 2-year Foundation Programme and several years of specialty training. In India, MBBS takes about 5.5 years, with optional specialization afterwards.

What subjects do I need for medical school?

Chemistry and biology are almost always required, and math and physics are often recommended. Entry is competitive, so it's worth focusing on these subjects early. Check each university's exact requirements.

Can I become a doctor later in life?

Yes. In the US many people enter medical school after another career, and the UK offers graduate-entry medicine courses. It's a long commitment, but life experience can be an advantage. Read more in how to change careers at 30+.

Will AI replace doctors?

AI already helps analyze scans and suggest possible diagnoses, but decisions, responsibility and conversations with patients stay with the doctor. Healthcare is one of the fields where job growth is expected in the coming years. Read more in jobs of the future and AI.
