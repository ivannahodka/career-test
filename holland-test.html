What is the Holland Code?

American psychologist John L. Holland (1919–2008) noticed that people in the same occupation tend to share similar interests and personalities. From decades of research he developed the theory that most people — and most work environments — can be described by a mix of six types. Their first letters form the acronym RIASEC.

Holland, a long-time professor at Johns Hopkins University, set out his theory in the book Making Vocational Choices and created the Self-Directed Search, one of the first career tests people could take and score on their own. Today his model underpins many career assessments, including the U.S. Department of Labor's O*NET Interest Profiler.

Holland arranged the six types on a hexagon. Types next to each other (like Realistic and Investigative) have a lot in common; types on opposite sides (like Realistic and Social) are the most different. A code made of neighboring letters, such as RIC or SAE, is called consistent and is very common.

How the test is scored

Each of the 48 activities belongs to one of the six types — eight per type. Your answers are scored from 1 (strongly dislike) to 5 (strongly like), so each type gets a score from 8 to 40. Your Holland code is made of your three highest-scoring types, from strongest to weakest.

  • The first letter is your dominant interest — the kind of work you're likely to enjoy most.
  • The second and third letters refine it. An "IA" person and an "IC" person both like analysis, but the first leans toward creative research and the second toward precise, structured work.
  • If two scores are tied, both orders are valid — explore careers for each.
The test measures interests, not abilities. A high Realistic score means you'd likely enjoy hands-on work, not that you're already good at it. Use your code to decide which careers to explore, then test them in practice — through courses, projects or job shadowing.

FAQ

What is a Holland Code?

A Holland Code is a three-letter summary of your strongest career interests, based on psychologist John Holland's RIASEC model: Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising and Conventional. For example, the code SAE means your strongest interest is Social, followed by Artistic and Enterprising.

How accurate is the RIASEC test?

The RIASEC model is one of the most researched frameworks in career psychology, and the activities in this test come from the O*NET Interest Profiler Short Form developed for the U.S. Department of Labor. Still, it measures interests, not abilities, so treat the result as a starting point for exploring careers rather than a final answer.

What age is the Holland Code test for?

It works for teenagers from about 13–14 and for adults. Interests are still developing in the teenage years, so it is worth retaking the test every year or two. Adults often use it when changing careers.

What if two of my scores are tied?

Ties are common. Look at careers that combine both types — for example, a UX designer combines Artistic and Investigative interests. You can swap the tied letters in your code and explore both versions.
